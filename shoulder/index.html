The Shoulder Lab

Ten steps, built in the order we think about it: bone first, then motion, then the muscle that moves it, then what sits on top, what holds it together, how it breaks down, how we test it, and how we train it. Every model is live. Grab it, spin it, move it.

Models are stylised and built to scale for teaching. Attachments and paths are simplified. Education and screening only, not a diagnosis. If something is painful, unstable, or numb, it goes to a clinician.

STEP 01

Bony structure first

Four bones and a rib cage. The humerus is a ball, the scapula is a moving socket, the clavicle is the only bony strut tying the arm to the trunk, and the thorax is the platform everything rides on. Learn the landmarks and the rest of the lesson has somewhere to attach.

The big idea

The glenoid is a shallow dish, roughly a golf ball on a tee. Almost nothing about the bone keeps the shoulder in. Muscle and soft tissue do that job, which is why they matter so much.

Four joints, one shoulder

Glenohumeral, acromioclavicular, sternoclavicular, and the scapulothoracic articulation (a functional joint, no capsule). A problem at any one changes the demand on the others.

Coach read

When an athlete can't reach, ask which of the four joints is not contributing before blaming the one that hurts.

STEP 02

Motion of the shoulder

The arm does not elevate at the glenohumeral joint alone. The scapula upwardly rotates, tilts back, and rotates externally while the clavicle elevates and rotates. Move the arm and watch the split between ball-and-socket and scapula.

Scapulohumeral rhythm

Roughly 2° at the glenohumeral joint for every 1° of scapular upward rotation across full elevation. About 120° GH plus 60° scapula for 180° overhead. Individuals vary, and the first 30° or so is mostly GH.

Turn the rhythm off

Freeze the scapula and push the arm up. The greater tubercle runs straight into the acromion. That's the picture behind subacromial pain.

Throwing

The arm is asked to go from about 165° of layback to ball release in a few hundredths of a second. Press Play throw and watch where each segment contributes.

STEP 03

The rotator cuff

Four muscles whose tendons blend into a cuff around the humeral head. SITS: supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis. They are as much a steering and centering system as a power source.

Origins stay, insertions move

Every cuff origin sits on the scapula, every insertion on the proximal humerus. The cuff therefore ties the moving socket to the moving ball.

Dynamic centering

The cuff compresses the head into the glenoid so the big movers (deltoid, pec, lat) can work without shearing the head upward or forward.

The throwing job

Infraspinatus and teres minor generate braking force in deceleration while the arm is still moving forward at speed. Subscapularis is the largest cuff muscle and a major internal rotator.

STEP 04

Placement: superficial to deep

What you see in the mirror is the top layer. What keeps the joint centered is two layers down. Peel the model back one layer at a time and see where the cuff sits relative to everything that covers it.

Layer 1

Superficial. Trapezius, deltoid, latissimus dorsi, pec major. Big, long levers. They set the shape of the shoulder and produce most of the torque.

Layer 2

Intermediate. Rhomboids, levator, serratus anterior, pec minor, teres major, biceps and triceps long heads. They position the scapula under the arm.

Layer 3

Deep. The rotator cuff. Short, close to the joint, built to hold position. Force couples are how layers 1 to 3 work as one system.

STEP 05

Tendon, ligament, cartilage

Three different tissues, three different jobs. Define them first, then see how each one shows up at the shoulder.

Passive vs active

Ligaments and capsule are passive restraints that matter at end range. Muscle-tendon units do the work in the middle of the range.

Rotate the arm

Use the position buttons and watch the glow move from the superior and middle ligaments toward the inferior band as the arm goes to 90° and rotates.

Healing speed

Muscle has a good blood supply. Tendon and ligament have less. Articular cartilage has essentially none. Loading plans respect that order.

STEP 06

Striations, fascia, and lines of pull

Muscle fibers are not all aimed the same way, and muscles are wrapped in connective tissue that links neighbors together. Fiber direction sets the line of pull. Fascial continuity gives us a map of how tension may travel across regions.

Fiber architecture

Strap, fusiform, convergent (fan), pennate, multipennate. Architecture decides force versus excursion, and how many different jobs one muscle can do.

Same muscle, different jobs

Upper, middle, and lower trapezius share a name and have different lines of pull. The same is true for the heads of pec major and the three parts of the deltoid.

Honest caveat

The line maps are a thinking tool. Force transmission through fascia is real and measurable in the lab. How much it matters for a given athlete is still being worked out.

STEP 07

Posture and what it points to

Posture is a snapshot. Movement is the test. Use the posture to decide which movements to look at, then let the movement tell you whether the posture matters for this athlete.

Don't pathologize

Plenty of high-level throwers look "bad" on the posture screen and move beautifully. If it doesn't change performance or symptoms, it's a trait, not a problem.

Posture to movement

Each pattern predicts a short list of movement checks. Do those, not a generic screen.

Rib cage is the base

A scapula on a stiff, flexed thoracic spine has less posterior tilt and upward rotation available. That's why thoracic mobility keeps showing up in shoulder plans.

STEP 08

Reading the shoulder: scapular and humeral syndromes

The same seven patterns from the VeloU shoulder exam. Know what normal looks like, match what you see to a syndrome, then inhibit, activate, use, and avoid accordingly. The left scapula in the model is the normal reference.

Static, then dynamic

Look at the resting position first, then watch the scapula as the arm moves and while it's loaded. Use the elevate slider and the load switch to see why.

Expect more than one

Athletes rarely show a single pattern. Pick drills so fixing one doesn't work against another.

Inhibit, then activate

Calm the overactive muscle first, then wake up the underactive one. No symptoms does not mean healthy.

STEP 09

What throwing does to the shoulder

Years of high-velocity throwing reshape the shoulder. Some of that is bone, some is soft tissue, and some is a smart adaptation. Telling them apart is the whole game.

The numbers from the research we've reviewed

Read these as patterns, not rules. Most of these are single studies, many are small, and several are observational. Individual baselines beat population thresholds.

ROM calculator

Bone adapts first

Throwers build extra humeral retroversion while the skeleton is still maturing. That shifts the arc toward ER and away from IR without changing the total.

Total arc is the number

Compare ER plus IR on the throwing side to the other side. A shifted arc with a matching total is an adaptation. A smaller total is the flag.

Acute vs chronic

Range drops after a bullpen and recovers. What matters is whether it stays down day after day.

STEP 10

Manual testing

Choose a test, watch the model get into position, and see where the examiner's force goes and what structure is being asked the question. Tests cluster. No single one makes the diagnosis.

Cluster, don't hunt

Use a short cluster for the question you're asking: cuff, subacromial, internal impingement, labrum, instability. Agreement across tests is what counts.

Compare sides

Test the good side first, same position, same pressure. Many athletes are lax or painful in a position that's normal for them.

Know when to refer

Night pain, numbness, a true giving way, a pop with weakness, or significant loss of strength gets sent on. We screen and train. We don't diagnose.
