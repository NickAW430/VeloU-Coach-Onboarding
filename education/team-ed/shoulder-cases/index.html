01 · On the floor

The pinchy press

Your athlete is pressing overhead. The bar comes off the rack fine, but around the middle of the press something catches. "It's pinchy on the side of my shoulder." At the top it eases off. Lowering the bar, it pinches again on the way down.

What you see and hear

  • Pain on the side or front of the shoulder, usually between about 60° and 120° of raising the arm (a "painful arc")
  • Worse with overhead press, lateral raises, upright rows, and sometimes wide-grip bench
  • Aches at night, especially lying on that side
  • Hard to reach behind the back or overhead on that side

How athletes compensate

  • Shrugging to get the arm up (upper trap takes over)
  • Arching the low back and flaring the ribs to fake overhead range
  • Bar drifts forward, elbows flare, or the press turns into an incline bench
  • Shortening the range, or avoiding overhead entirely
02 · What's going on

A crowded space under the roof of the shoulder

Raise your arm and the top of the humerus has to pass under a bony roof (the acromion). Between them sit the supraspinatus tendon, the subacromial bursa, and part of the biceps tendon. The gap is only about a centimeter. The most common reason that gap gets tight on the floor is poor upward rotation of the shoulder blade. As the arm goes up, the blade should turn so the socket faces the ceiling and the acromion swings up and out of the way. When it doesn't, the humerus runs into the roof, and the tissues in between take a beating, especially through mid-range where the space is smallest. A cuff that can't keep the ball centered makes it worse.

The takeaway: look at the shoulder blade first. If it isn't rotating up, the roof doesn't get out of the way and the cuff pays for it. Cuff capacity and load still matter, since the tendon is irritated when it's asked to do more than it's prepared for in a tight position. But upward rotation is usually the first thing to find and fix.

Try it: the gap under the roof

Who turns the blade up, and who turns it down

Raising the arm is a team effort between the arm bone and the shoulder blade. The blade has to rotate upward, and a group of muscles on the back of the trunk does that. Another group works against it, rotating the blade downward. Healthy shoulders keep the two in balance.

Upward rotators

  • Upper trapezius: lifts the outer end of the collarbone and acromion
  • Lower trapezius: pulls the inner edge of the blade down and in, and is the key to finishing the rotation
  • Serratus anterior: pulls the lower tip of the blade forward and up around the ribs

Downward rotators

  • Rhomboids: pull the inner edge up and in, tipping the socket down
  • Levator scapulae: lifts the upper corner of the blade
  • Pec minor: tips the blade forward and down. The lats also pull the blade down through the arm.
Go deeper: why the name keeps changing

You'll hear "impingement," "subacromial pain," "rotator cuff tendinopathy," and "rotator cuff–related shoulder pain." Current thinking prefers the last one. The tendon is often irritated by load, and it isn't always being pinched. The tests we use can't tell these apart reliably, which is why we cluster them, and why the treatment answer is usually the same: manage load, restore the movement, and build capacity.

Go deeper: the scapulohumeral rhythm

For every 3° of arm elevation, about 2° comes from the glenohumeral joint and 1° from the scapula rotating upward. That rhythm is what keeps the acromion out of the way. Look from behind while the athlete raises the arm: a shoulder blade that shrugs early, winds up late, or tips forward (anterior tilt) is changing the size of the space.

  • Upper trap dominance shows as an early shrug.
  • Serratus or lower trap weakness shows as less upward rotation and a prominent inferior angle.
  • Tight pec minor and lats pull the scapula forward and down, so the arm can't get overhead cleanly.
04 · Confirm it

A short cluster, not one test

Pick a test and the model moves into the position. The red arrow is the examiner's force and the lit muscles are the ones being asked the question. "Show a positive response" and the sweep button are on the right.

Written steps for all five tests
Painful arcAthlete raises the arm out to the side (or in the scapular plane) and back down.Positive: pain mostly through the middle range (about 60–120°), easier at the start and end.
Scapular assistance testAthlete raises the arm. Watch where it hurts or catches. Repeat while you gently help the shoulder blade turn up and back (hand on the lower tip of the blade, other hand on the top).Positive: the pinch eases or the arm rises more easily when you help the blade.
Hawkins–KennedyArm at 90° forward, elbow bent. Examiner gently rotates the forearm down (internal rotation).Positive: pain at the front or side of the shoulder.
NeerExaminer holds the scapula and passively raises the arm in the scapular plane with the arm turned in.Positive: pain at the end of the raise.
Resisted external rotation at the sideElbow at the side, bent 90°. Athlete pushes out against your hand.Positive: pain or weakness compared to the other side.
The one that points at the cause: in the scapular assistance test, if helping the blade turn up eases the pinch, poor upward rotation is part of what's going on. That tells you what to train. Also watch from behind as the athlete raises both arms: an early shrug, or a blade that lags or tips forward on the sore side, tells you the same thing.
How much to trust the others: Hawkins and Neer are sensitive but not specific. Plenty of front-of-shoulder problems hurt in those positions. Painful arc, Hawkins–Kennedy, and weak or painful resisted external rotation together tell you much more than any one alone. If the cluster is negative but the athlete is still sore, keep looking (neck, AC joint, biceps, instability).

05 · Clear it

Is a downward rotator holding them back?

Before you blame the cuff, ask whether the blade can turn up at all. Three common patterns park the shoulder blade in some level of downward rotation: military posture, scapular depression, and scapular downward rotation itself. Each limits overhead movement a little differently, so the prep for each is different. Pick a pattern below to see it on the model, how to tell if it's the thing in the way, and how to prepare for pressing around it.

The clearing routine (about two minutes)

  1. Baseline. Pick one number to beat: the angle where the pinch starts, the height the athlete can reach overhead, or pain on a 0–10 scale during an empty-bar press.
  2. Look. Standing, from behind and from the side. Which pattern is closest?
  3. Test. Watch the blade as they raise both arms. Run the scapular assistance test. Check thoracic rotation and push-up plus for military posture.
  4. Prep. 30–60 seconds to calm the short muscles, then 5–8 slow reps to wake up the weak ones (the lists above).
  5. Retest the same baseline. Better means the downward rotators or the posture were in the way, and you now have your prep for the day. No change means look elsewhere: thoracic extension, the cuff, the joint itself.
Keep it honest: posture on its own doesn't tell you who will hurt, and a quiet shoulder isn't proof of a healthy one. What matters is whether the prep changes how the athlete moves and feels right now. If pain doesn't change, or any referral flag from the last section is present, stop and refer.

06 · What to do

Keep them training, change what bothers them

Adjust today

  • Stay pain-free to mild and settling within a day. Don't push through sharp pain.
  • Press in the scapular plane (arm slightly in front of the body) instead of straight out to the side
  • Swap strict overhead for landmine press or a high-incline press with neutral grip
  • Narrow the bench grip, use a floor press or board press, and cut depth if the painful range is the bottom
  • Drop volume and pause upright rows and lateral-raise volume above 90°

Build over the next weeks

  • Upward rotation first: serratus and lower trap work such as wall slides, push-up plus, and scapular-plane raises with the blade moving up and around the ribs
  • Load the cuff progressively. Start with isometric external rotation at the side if movement hurts.
  • Thoracic extension and rotation work. Restore overhead without the low-back arch.
  • Soften what's tight: pec minor, lats, posterior shoulder
  • Reassess in 1–2 weeks: arc, strength, and how overhead feels
Stop and refer to the athletic trainer, physical therapist, or physician if any of these show up: pain after a fall or sudden "pop"; can't lift the arm; a clear drop in strength; numbness or tingling into the hand; neck pain that reproduces the arm symptoms; pain that wakes them up every night; fever, swelling, or deformity; chest pain or shortness of breath with arm pain (call emergency services); no change after a couple of weeks of sensible changes.

01 · On the floor

The tight back of the shoulder

A pitcher is three weeks into the throwing program. Velocity is down a couple of ticks late in the session, and command fades in the last 15 throws. "My shoulder's tight in the back, and I can't get it to loosen up." Sleeping on that side is uncomfortable. Their arm feels late through the layback.

What you see and hear

  • Achy or tight back of the shoulder that doesn't settle after throwing
  • Less internal rotation than the other arm, and often less cross-body reach
  • Velocity or command slips as the session goes on
  • Arm feels late, heavy, or "stuck" at the top of layback
  • Shoulder blade sits forward and tipped on the throwing side

How athletes compensate

  • Lean the trunk to the glove side or open early to get the arm through
  • Lay the arm back too far (horizontal abduction) to find range
  • Rush the arm, leaving the lower body behind
  • Lose follow-through length
02 · What's going on

A shifted arc is normal. A smaller arc is the flag.

Years of throwing reshape the shoulder. The humerus twists back (retroversion), so throwers gain external rotation and lose internal rotation without losing total motion. That's an adaptation, and it isn't the problem. The problem is a tight posterior capsule and cuff that shrinks the total arc. Then the ball glides forward and up, the scapula protracts to make up for it, and the back of the shoulder takes more load.

The takeaway: don't chase internal rotation alone. Compare total arc (ER + IR) on both sides.

Watch where the throw loads the back of the shoulder

Play the throw or pick a phase. The lit muscles are the ones doing the main work in that phase, and the glowing band is the posterior capsule. The big braking load comes in deceleration, after release.

Try it: bone or soft tissue?

Go deeper: what the numbers mean
  • IR deficit (non-throwing IR minus throwing IR) beyond about 18–20° is the traditional "GIRD" flag.
  • Total arc deficit of about 5° or more compared to the other arm is the number that matters more.
  • These are thresholds that get your attention, not diagnoses. The athlete's own baseline beats population numbers.
  • Range drops after throwing and recovers. It matters when it stays down day after day.
  • In youth players, the growth plate of the upper arm is a weak link. Pain there gets referred (see below).
What the research says, and how much to trust it
  • Total arc and ER: in 160 left-handed high school pitchers, those with 109° or more of passive ER were 3.3 times more likely to have a shoulder or elbow injury. The injured group also had a bigger total arc (166° vs 156°). More motion isn't automatically better. Both extremes are worth a note.
  • Acute loss: after 100 full-effort pitches, IR range was down and stayed down for at least 24 hours. Check range across days, not once.
  • Strength over range: in collegiate pitchers, ER and IR isometric strength tracked velocity (r = .61 and .56) and range of motion didn't.
  • Findings aren't symptoms: in asymptomatic elite pitching prospects, 93.9% had rotator cuff abnormalities and 75.8% had labral tears on imaging. Judge the athlete in front of you.
  • A quick screen: posterior capsule thickness by ultrasound matched MRI in 25 drafted pitchers (r = .945).
  • Strength comparison: position players had 14–20% more ER and IR strength than pitchers on the throwing arm.

These are mostly single studies, many small or observational. Read them as patterns, not rules, and let an athlete's own baseline beat any population number.

Patterns to look for

Tight posterior tissue rarely travels alone. These four patterns show up with it. Pick one to see it on the model, how to confirm it, and what to inhibit, activate and use. Use the elevation slider to see how the blade behaves as the arm goes up.

Four rules for using these

  • Expect more than one pattern. Choose drills so fixing one doesn't work against another.
  • Inhibit the overactive muscle before you activate the underactive one.
  • Watch how global lifts affect the corrective work, like heavy carries and deadlifts.
  • No symptoms doesn't mean healthy.

05 · What to do

Restore the arc, support the scapula, watch the load

The sleeper stretch at three angles

Pick an angle and the model takes the arm position. Changing the angle moves the stretch to a different part of the posterior shoulder. The glowing band is the posterior capsule. In the real stretch the athlete is side-lying and the table holds the shoulder blade.

Adjust today

  • Measure ROM before and after a session. Is the drop recovering by the next day?
  • Sleeper stretch at three angles: 120°, 90° and 60° of abduction (see the model above). Find where it's tight and spend the time there.
  • Soft-tissue work on the back of the shoulder (ball or manual release) before throwing
  • If velocity or command fade late, cut the volume before you chase mechanics
  • Keep the arm warm and the scapula engaged in the throwing warm-up

Build over the next weeks

  • Eccentric ER and cuff endurance for the deceleration job
  • Serratus and lower trap work to keep the socket under the ball
  • Thoracic rotation and hip mobility (lead hip internal rotation) so the arm isn't doing the trunk's work
  • Check stride length, trunk tilt, and early opening when arm speed fades
  • Re-measure weekly. Total arc should stay level with the other side.
Stop and refer to the athletic trainer, physical therapist, or physician if: pain with throwing lasts more than a few days or keeps getting worse; a sudden "pop" or sharp pain during a throw; clicking, catching, or the arm feeling like it slips; swelling; numbness or tingling in the hand; elbow pain along with the shoulder; velocity doesn't come back after a rest block; or a youth thrower with pain in the upper arm just below the shoulder (possible growth-plate stress). Total arc is more than 5° smaller than the other side and not recovering.
