01 · On the floor

The pinchy press

Your athlete is pressing overhead. The bar comes off the rack fine, but around the middle of the press something catches. "It's pinchy on the side of my shoulder." At the top it eases off. Lowering the bar, it pinches again on the way down.

What you see and hear

  • Pain on the side or front of the shoulder, usually between about 60° and 120° of raising the arm (a "painful arc")
  • Worse with overhead press, lateral raises, upright rows, and sometimes wide-grip bench
  • Aches at night, especially lying on that side
  • Hard to reach behind the back or overhead on that side

How athletes compensate

  • Shrugging to get the arm up (upper trap takes over)
  • Arching the low back and flaring the ribs to fake overhead range
  • Bar drifts forward, elbows flare, or the press turns into an incline bench
  • Shortening the range, or avoiding overhead entirely
02 · What's going on

A crowded space under the roof of the shoulder

Raise your arm and the top of the humerus has to pass under a bony roof (the acromion). Between them sit the supraspinatus tendon, the subacromial bursa, and part of the biceps tendon. The gap is only about a centimeter. When the shoulder blade doesn't rotate up enough, or the rotator cuff doesn't hold the ball centered, that gap gets tighter and those tissues take a beating, especially at mid-range where the space is smallest.

The takeaway: pain here is usually a load problem: tissues asked to do more than they've been prepared for, in a position where the space is tight. It is rarely just "one bad muscle."

Go deeper: why the name keeps changing

You'll hear "impingement," "subacromial pain," "rotator cuff tendinopathy," and "rotator cuff–related shoulder pain." Current thinking prefers the last one. The tendon is often irritated by load, and it isn't always being pinched. The tests we use can't tell these apart reliably, which is why we cluster them, and why the treatment answer is usually the same: manage load, restore the movement, and build capacity.

Go deeper: the scapulohumeral rhythm

For every 3° of arm elevation, about 2° comes from the glenohumeral joint and 1° from the scapula rotating upward. That rhythm is what keeps the acromion out of the way. Look from behind while the athlete raises the arm: a shoulder blade that shrugs early, winds up late, or tips forward (anterior tilt) is changing the size of the space.

  • Upper trap dominance shows as an early shrug.
  • Serratus or lower trap weakness shows as less upward rotation and a prominent inferior angle.
  • Tight pec minor and lats pull the scapula forward and down, so the arm can't get overhead cleanly.
04 · Confirm it

A short cluster, not one test

Painful arcAthlete raises the arm out to the side (or in the scapular plane) and back down.Positive: pain mostly through the middle range (about 60–120°), easier at the start and end.
Hawkins–KennedyArm at 90° forward, elbow bent. Examiner gently rotates the forearm down (internal rotation).Positive: pain at the front or side of the shoulder.
NeerExaminer holds the scapula and passively raises the arm in the scapular plane with the arm turned in.Positive: pain at the end of the raise.
Resisted external rotation at the sideElbow at the side, bent 90°. Athlete pushes out against your hand.Positive: pain or weakness compared to the other side.
How much to trust them: Hawkins and Neer are sensitive but not specific. Plenty of front-of-shoulder problems hurt in those positions. Painful arc, Hawkins–Kennedy, and weak or painful resisted external rotation together tell you much more than any one alone. If the cluster is negative but the athlete is still sore, keep looking (neck, AC joint, biceps, instability).

05 · What to do

Keep them training, change what bothers them

Adjust today

  • Stay pain-free to mild and settling within a day. Don't push through sharp pain.
  • Press in the scapular plane (arm slightly in front of the body) instead of straight out to the side
  • Swap strict overhead for landmine press or a high-incline press with neutral grip
  • Narrow the bench grip, use a floor press or board press, and cut depth if the painful range is the bottom
  • Drop volume and pause upright rows and lateral-raise volume above 90°

Build over the next weeks

  • Load the cuff progressively. Start with isometric external rotation at the side if movement hurts.
  • Serratus and lower trap: wall slides, push-up plus, scapular-plane raises
  • Thoracic extension and rotation work. Restore overhead without the low-back arch.
  • Soften what's tight: pec minor, lats, posterior shoulder
  • Reassess in 1–2 weeks: arc, strength, and how overhead feels
Stop and refer to the athletic trainer, physical therapist, or physician if any of these show up: pain after a fall or sudden "pop"; can't lift the arm; a clear drop in strength; numbness or tingling into the hand; neck pain that reproduces the arm symptoms; pain that wakes them up every night; fever, swelling, or deformity; chest pain or shortness of breath with arm pain (call emergency services); no change after a couple of weeks of sensible changes.

01 · On the floor

The tight back of the shoulder

A pitcher is three weeks into the throwing program. Velocity is down a couple of ticks late in the session, and command fades in the last 15 throws. "My shoulder's tight in the back, and I can't get it to loosen up." Sleeping on that side is uncomfortable. Their arm feels late through the layback.

What you see and hear

  • Achy or tight back of the shoulder that doesn't settle after throwing
  • Less internal rotation than the other arm, and often less cross-body reach
  • Velocity or command slips as the session goes on
  • Arm feels late, heavy, or "stuck" at the top of layback
  • Shoulder blade sits forward and tipped on the throwing side

How athletes compensate

  • Lean the trunk to the glove side or open early to get the arm through
  • Lay the arm back too far (horizontal abduction) to find range
  • Rush the arm, leaving the lower body behind
  • Lose follow-through length
02 · What's going on

A shifted arc is normal. A smaller arc is the flag.

Years of throwing reshape the shoulder. The humerus twists back (retroversion), so throwers gain external rotation and lose internal rotation without losing total motion. That's an adaptation, and it isn't the problem. The problem is a tight posterior capsule and cuff that shrinks the total arc. Then the ball glides forward and up, the scapula protracts to make up for it, and the back of the shoulder takes more load.

The takeaway: don't chase internal rotation alone. Compare total arc (ER + IR) on both sides.

Go deeper: what the numbers mean
  • IR deficit (non-throwing IR minus throwing IR) beyond about 18–20° is the traditional "GIRD" flag.
  • Total arc deficit of about 5° or more compared to the other arm is the number that matters more.
  • These are thresholds that get your attention, not diagnoses. The athlete's own baseline beats population numbers.
  • Range drops after throwing and recovers. It matters when it stays down day after day.
  • In youth players, the growth plate of the upper arm is a weak link. Pain there gets referred (see below).

04 · Confirm it

Measure both sides, then check the scapula

ER and IR range, both armsAthlete lies on their back, arm at 90° out, elbow bent 90°. Hold the shoulder blade still with one hand and rotate with the other. Measure the angle at end range with a goniometer or phone level. Same person, same method, both sides.Look for: total arc (ER + IR) smaller than the other arm by about 5° or more, or an IR deficit that keeps growing.
Cross-body reach (horizontal adduction)Arm at 90° forward, scapula held. Bring the arm across the chest. Measure the distance from the elbow to the opposite side.Look for: a clear difference from the other side.
Scapular dyskinesis testAthlete raises and lowers the arm with light weights. Watch from behind.Positive: winging, early shrug, or uneven motion compared to the other side.
Posterior impingement sign and relocationArm laid back at 90°/90° (the cocked position). Examiner adds gentle pressure forward, then a posterior hold.Positive: deep back-of-shoulder pain that eases with the hold. Some contact is normal in throwers. Reproducing their pain is the point.
Careful: these are screening tools. Pain with the layback tests, a clicking or catching shoulder, or any instability feeling moves this from "screen and manage" to "refer."

05 · What to do

Restore the arc, support the scapula, watch the load

Adjust today

  • Measure ROM before and after a session. Is the drop recovering by the next day?
  • Cross-body stretch with the scapula held, and a gentle sleeper stretch (stay out of pinching; stop if it hurts at the front)
  • Soft-tissue work on the back of the shoulder (ball or manual release) before throwing
  • If velocity or command fade late, cut the volume before you chase mechanics
  • Keep the arm warm and the scapula engaged in the throwing warm-up

Build over the next weeks

  • Eccentric ER and cuff endurance for the deceleration job
  • Serratus and lower trap work to keep the socket under the ball
  • Thoracic rotation and hip mobility (lead hip internal rotation) so the arm isn't doing the trunk's work
  • Check stride length, trunk tilt, and early opening when arm speed fades
  • Re-measure weekly. Total arc should stay level with the other side.
Stop and refer to the athletic trainer, physical therapist, or physician if: pain with throwing lasts more than a few days or keeps getting worse; a sudden "pop" or sharp pain during a throw; clicking, catching, or the arm feeling like it slips; swelling; numbness or tingling in the hand; elbow pain along with the shoulder; velocity doesn't come back after a rest block; or a youth thrower with pain in the upper arm just below the shoulder (possible growth-plate stress). Total arc is more than 5° smaller than the other side and not recovering.
